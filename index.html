Introduction: What is NLP?

Levels of Language Processing in NLP & the NLP Pipeline

Natural Language Processing (NLP) is a field of Artificial Intelligence that enables computers to understand, interpret, generate, and respond to human language, in both text and speech. Everyday examples: Google Translate, chatbots, voice assistants (Siri, Alexa), spam filters, autocorrect, and search engines.

Human language is complex and ambiguous, so NLP breaks it down into levels. Each level adds a deeper layer of understanding, from raw sounds and word parts up to the speaker’s real intent. To actually build an NLP system, the work is organized into a step-by-step pipeline (Part 2).

Memory aid for the 7 levels (in order): “Pink Monkeys Love Sweet Strawberries During Picnics” = Phonological, Morphological, Lexical, Syntactic, Semantic, Discourse, Pragmatic.
Part 1 · Levels of Language Processing

Part 1 Overview: The 7 Levels

LevelDescriptionExampleApplications
PhonologicalStudy of speech sounds: how sounds are produced, organized, and pronounced (phonemes, pronunciation, prosody).“cat” = /k/ /æ/ /t/Speech recognition, text-to-speech, voice assistants

LexicalStudy of individual words: their meanings, categories, and relationships to other words.“bank” (money) vs “bank” (river)Dictionaries, thesauri, POS tagging, search
SyntacticStudy of sentence structure: how words are arranged into phrases and sentences following grammar rules.“The cat chased the mouse” (Subject–Verb–Object)Grammar checkers, parsing, machine translation

Note: The phonological level applies to spoken language. Text-only NLP systems (e.g., a text chatbot) usually start from the morphological level. Some textbooks group the lexical level under semantics; the two are closely related.
Part 1 · Level 1 of 7

1. Phonological Level

Deals with the sound system of a language. It is the first level for any system that listens to or produces speech.

Phonemes

  • DefinitionThe smallest unit of sound in a spoken language that can change the meaning of a word.
  • Example“bat” and “pat” differ only by one phoneme (/b/ vs /p/). The word “cat” has three phonemes: /k/ /æ/ /t/. English has about 44 phonemes.
  • ApplicationSpeech recognition models convert audio into phonemes and then into words; text-to-speech systems do the reverse.

Phonetic Transcription

  • DefinitionA written representation of how a word is actually pronounced, usually using the International Phonetic Alphabet (IPA). Needed because English spelling does not always match pronunciation.
  • Example“knight” → /naɪt/ (the k and gh are silent). “though” → /ðoʊ/, “through” → /θruː/, “tough” → /tʌf/ (same letters “ough,” three different sounds).
  • ApplicationPronunciation dictionaries, grapheme-to-phoneme conversion in text-to-speech, and language-learning apps.

Prosody

  • DefinitionThe rhythm, stress, intonation, pitch, and speed of speech. It carries emotion and sentence type beyond the words themselves.
  • ExampleRising pitch turns “You’re coming.” into a question: “You’re coming?” Stress changes meaning: “I didn’t steal the money” vs “I didn’t steal the money.”
  • ApplicationNatural-sounding voice assistants, emotion detection in call centers, and detecting questions or sarcasm in speech.
Part 1 · Level 2 of 7

2. Morphological Level

Deals with the internal structure of words. Knowing how words are built helps a system recognize related words (run, runs, running, runner) as variations of one idea.

Morphemes

  • DefinitionThe smallest unit of a language that carries meaning or grammatical function. (Not the same as a syllable.)
  • Example“unbreakable” = un- (not) + break (root) + -able (can be). That is 3 morphemes. “dogs” = dog + -s (plural). Types: free morphemes can stand alone (book, run); bound morphemes cannot (un-, -ed, -s, -ness).
  • ApplicationSubword tokenization, stemming, and handling languages with rich word-building such as Filipino (sulat → sumulat, nagsulat, pagsulat-an) and Korean.

Inflection

  • DefinitionChanging a word’s form to show grammatical information (tense, number, person, comparison, aspect) without changing its core meaning or part of speech. It applies to verbs, nouns, and adjectives, not only verb tense.
  • Examplewalk → walked (past tense); cat → cats (plural); big → bigger (comparison). Filipino: sulat → sumulat (completed aspect).
  • ApplicationLemmatization maps inflected forms back to one dictionary form; search engines match “running” with “run.”

Derivation

  • DefinitionCreating a new word (often with a new meaning or a new part of speech) by adding prefixes or suffixes.
  • Examplehappy (adjective) → happiness (noun); teach (verb) → teacher (noun); kind → unkind (opposite meaning).
  • ApplicationVocabulary expansion, stemming, and helping machine translation handle unseen words by recognizing their parts.

Part 1 · Level 3 of 7

3. Lexical Level

Deals with individual words and the lexicon (the vocabulary of a language: each word with its part of speech, meanings, and forms).

Lexical Semantics

  • DefinitionThe study of the meaning of individual words and the relationships between words. A single word can have several senses, and different words can share or oppose meanings.
  • Example“bank” can mean a financial institution or a river edge. WordNet, a well-known lexical database, groups words into sets of synonyms (synsets).
  • ApplicationSearch engines expanding queries with synonyms, thesauri, sentiment lexicons (lists of positive/negative words), word embeddings, and part-of-speech (POS) tagging.
RelationMeaningExample
SynonymyWords with similar meaningbig / large
AntonymyWords with opposite meaninghot / cold
Hypernymy / HyponymyGeneral term (hypernym) and its specific type (hyponym)animal → dog
MeronymyPart–whole relationshipwheel is part of a car

HomonymySame spelling/sound, unrelated meanings“bat” (animal) / “bat” (baseball)

Related task, POS tagging: labeling each word with its grammatical category. Example: The/DET cat/NOUN sleeps/VERB.

Part 1 · Level 4 of 7

4. Syntactic Level

Deals with sentence structure: the rules for arranging words into phrases and valid sentences. “The dog bites the man” and “The man bites the dog” have the same words but different structure and meaning.

Parsing

  • DefinitionAnalyzing the grammatical structure of a sentence and the relationships among its words, producing a parse tree or dependency graph.
  • Example“The cat chased the mouse.” is parsed into a subject (The cat), a verb (chased), and an object (the mouse). Syntactic ambiguity: “I saw the man with the telescope” has two valid parses (who has the telescope?).
  • ApplicationGrammar checkers (e.g., Grammarly), machine translation, information extraction, and question answering.

Subject–Verb–Object (SVO)

  • DefinitionA basic word-order pattern: the Subject (who does the action) comes first, then the Verb (the action), then the Object (who or what receives the action). English is mostly SVO; other languages order words differently.
  • ExampleEnglish (SVO): “Maria (S) eats (V) rice (O).” Filipino is typically verb-first: “Kumain (V) si Maria (S) ng kanin (O).” Korean is SOV: 마리아가 (S) 밥을 (O) 먹어요 (V).
  • ApplicationMachine translation must reorder words between languages; parsers use word order to identify subject and object.

Constituency

  • DefinitionThe principle that groups of words function together as a single grammatical unit (a constituent, such as a noun phrase or verb phrase) to build the hierarchical structure of a sentence. Constituency parsing breaks a sentence into nested phrases.
  • Example“The cat chased the mouse” as a bracketed tree:
  • ApplicationSyntax-based machine translation, grammar checking, and extracting phrases (e.g., all noun phrases) from text.

Dependency

  • DefinitionThe grammatical relationship between words, where one word (the head or governor) governs or describes another (the dependent or modifier). Every word depends on exactly one head, and the main verb is the root.
  • ExampleIn “The cat chased the mouse”: chased is the root; cat → chased (subject); mouse → chased (object); each the → its noun (determiner).
  • ApplicationRelation extraction, sentiment analysis (which adjective describes which noun), and tools like spaCy and Stanford parser.

Part 1 · Level 5 of 7

5. Semantic Level

Deals with meaning: determining what words and sentences actually mean, not just how they are built. A sentence can be grammatically perfect but meaningless (“Colorless green ideas sleep furiously”).

Word Sense Disambiguation (WSD)

  • DefinitionDetermining which meaning of an ambiguous word is intended, based on its surrounding context. This is how a model “understands” a word through context.
  • Example“She deposited the check at the bank” (financial institution) vs “They sat by the river bank” (shore). “I saw a bat fly out of the cave” (animal) vs “He swung the bat” (sports).
  • ApplicationMachine translation (choosing the correct word in the target language), search relevance, and chatbots. Modern contextual models like BERT do this automatically.

Semantic Role Labeling (SRL)

  • DefinitionIdentifying the predicate (action) and its arguments, answering “who did what to whom, when, where, and how.” Roles include the agent (doer), the patient/theme (receiver), the recipient, time, and place.
  • Example“Maria gave Juan a book yesterday.” → Predicate: gave; Agent: Maria; Recipient: Juan; Theme: a book; Time: yesterday. Same roles even in passive form: “A book was given to Juan by Maria.”
  • ApplicationQuestion answering, information extraction, and summarization.

Named Entity Recognition (NER)

  • DefinitionFinding and classifying named entities in text into predefined categories such as person, organization, location, date, time, money, and percentage.
  • Example“Jose Rizal was born in Calamba, Laguna on June 19, 1861.” → Jose Rizal = PERSON; Calamba, Laguna = LOCATION; June 19, 1861 = DATE. Challenge: “Washington” may be a person, a place, or an organization depending on context.
  • ApplicationNews tagging, resume parsing, chatbots, search engines, customer-support ticket routing, and building knowledge graphs.
Part 1 · Level 6 of 7

6. Discourse Level

Goes beyond a single sentence. It studies how sentences relate to one another to form a meaningful paragraph, essay, or conversation. The meaning of a sentence can depend on the sentences before it.

Coherence

  • DefinitionThe meaning-based flow of a text. Sentences must make sense together and be logically related (cause–effect, contrast, elaboration, sequence). The second sentence should be connected to the first.
  • ExampleCoherent: “Ana was hungry. She ordered pizza.” Incoherent: “Ana was hungry. The moon orbits the Earth.”
  • ApplicationEvaluating essays, text summarization, and generating long text that stays on topic.

Cohesion

  • DefinitionThe surface-level linguistic links that tie sentences together, such as transition words (however, therefore, in addition), pronouns, repetition, and synonyms. Cohesion is the “glue” in the wording; coherence is the logic in the meaning.
  • Example“Ana studied hard. Therefore, she passed. However, she did not get the highest score.”
  • ApplicationWriting assistants, automated essay scoring, and text generation.

Anaphora Resolution

  • DefinitionDetermining which earlier word or phrase (the antecedent) a pronoun or referring expression points back to. A closely related task, coreference resolution, links all mentions of the same entity.
  • Example“Maria told Ana that she won.” (Who is “she”? ambiguous). “The trophy didn’t fit in the suitcase because it was too big.” (it = trophy; if “too small,” it = suitcase).
  • ApplicationSummarization, machine translation (pronouns differ by gender and language), multi-turn chatbots, and question answering.
Part 1 · Level 7 of 7

7. Pragmatic Level

The highest level. It studies language in real-world context: the speaker’s intention, the situation, and shared knowledge. The same words can mean different things in different situations.

Speech Acts

  • DefinitionThe idea that utterances perform actions, such as requesting, promising, warning, or apologizing, and not only stating facts. (Theory by Austin and Searle.)
  • Example“Can you pass the salt?” is a request, not a question about ability. “I promise to come” performs a promise.
  • ApplicationIntent detection in chatbots (“Book me a flight” → booking intent), voice assistants, and email triage.
  • Assertive: states facts (“It’s raining.”)
  • Directive: gets someone to act (“Close the door.”)
  • Commissive: commits the speaker (“I will help you.”)
  • Expressive: shows feelings (“Thank you!”)
  • Declarative: changes a status (“You’re hired.”)

Deixis (Deictic Words)

  • Definition“Pointing” words whose meaning depends on who says them, where, and when: person (I, you, we), place (here, there, this, that), and time (now, today, tomorrow). They are not the same as prepositions.
  • Example“Meet me here tomorrow.” has no fixed meaning until you know where the speaker is and what today’s date is.
  • ApplicationVirtual assistants (“remind me tomorrow”), chatbots tracking conversation context, and machine translation.

Contextual Understanding

  • DefinitionInterpreting the intended meaning of an utterance using the situation, speaker intent, tone, and background (world) knowledge, going beyond the literal words.
  • Example“It’s cold in here.” may really mean “Please close the window.” “Great, another Monday!” is usually sarcasm, not joy.
  • ApplicationSarcasm and sentiment detection, customer-support bots, and large language models that handle multi-turn conversations.
Part 2 · NLP Pipeline

Part 2 Overview: The NLP Pipeline

The NLP pipeline is the step-by-step workflow for turning raw language data into a working, maintained NLP system.

Flow: 1 Data Gathering → 2 Text Cleaning → 3 Text Preprocessing → 4 Feature Engineering → 5 Modeling → 6 Evaluation → 7 Deployment → 8 Monitoring & Model Updating (and back to earlier steps when the model needs improvement).
Part 2 · Step 1 of 8

1. Data Gathering (Input)

Collecting textual data from different sources that the model will learn from. Sources include websites (web scraping), APIs, social media, surveys, emails, customer reviews, company databases, chat logs, and public datasets (corpora). The data used to train the model determines what it can learn.

Part 2 · Step 2 of 8

2. Text Cleaning

Removing noise from the text, correcting spelling errors, and normalizing it so the data is consistent. This includes handling misspellings, slang, and abbreviations.

Noise in NLP

Noise is any part of the text that does not help the task: special characters, punctuation marks, numbers, HTML tags, URLs, emojis, and extra spaces. Removing noise means removing or fixing these. (Be careful: numbers, punctuation, and emojis are sometimes useful, e.g., prices or sentiment, so decide based on the task.)

Normalization

  • DefinitionConverting text into a standard form so that different versions of the same thing are treated the same. It involves deciding which version of a word to use.
  • Example“gonna” → “going to”; “2moro” → “tomorrow”; “colour” and “color” → one chosen spelling; “U.S.A.” and “USA” → “USA”; Filipino text slang “d2” → “dito.”
  • Why it mattersWithout it, the model treats “color” and “colour” as two different words and wastes learning on spelling variations.
Part 2 · Step 3 of 8

3. Text Preprocessing

Preparing the cleaned text so that a model can learn from it efficiently. Benefits:

  • Enhances accuracy: cleaner input leads to better predictions.
  • Reduces noise: removes details that distract the model.
  • Facilitates consistency: similar text is represented in the same way.
  • Improves training efficiency: a smaller, simpler vocabulary trains faster.
  • Boosts model quality: the model learns real patterns, not formatting quirks.
  • Mitigates bias: reduces unintended bias from inconsistent representation (it does not remove bias in the data itself).

a. Tokenization (sentence | word)

  • DefinitionSplitting text into smaller pieces called tokens: paragraphs into sentences, and sentences into words. Tokens are later mapped to numbers (an ID per word in the vocabulary) so the model can process them.
  • Example“NLP is fun. It is useful.” → Sentences: [“NLP is fun.”, “It is useful.”]. Words: [NLP, is, fun, ., It, is, useful, .]
  • Watch outSentence tokenizers stop at periods, but not every period ends a sentence (Dr. Cruz, 3.14, e.g.). Modern models (BERT, GPT) use subword tokenization (e.g., “unhappiness” → un + happi + ness).

b. Lowercasing

  • DefinitionConverting all text to lowercase so “Hello,” “HELLO,” and “hello” are one token.
  • Example“The Cat Sat” → “the cat sat”.
  • Watch outCase can carry meaning: “Apple” (company) vs “apple” (fruit), “US” vs “us.”

c. Stop Word Removal

  • DefinitionRemoving very common words that add little meaning, such as articles, prepositions, and auxiliary verbs (the, is, a, an, of, in, to).
  • Example“The cat is on the mat” → “cat mat”.
  • Watch outMeaning can be lost. “This is not good” → “good” flips the sentiment. Often kept for translation, sentiment analysis, and large language models; useful for search and topic modeling.

d. Normalization

  • DefinitionConverting text to a standard form and correcting spelling errors, so variants map to one form (expanding contractions, fixing misspellings, removing accents, standardizing numbers and dates).
  • Example“can’t” → “cannot”; “recieve” → “receive”; “café” → “cafe”.

4. Feature Engineering

  • DefinitionConverting the preprocessed text into numerical features (vectors) that a machine learning model can understand, since models work with numbers, not words. Also includes creating or selecting useful features.
TechniqueIdeaExample
Bag of Words (BoW)Counts how many times each word appears; ignores word order.Vocabulary [I, love, NLP, AI]. “I love NLP” → [1, 1, 1, 0]; “I love AI” → [1, 1, 0, 1]
TF-IDFWeights words by how important they are: frequent in a document but rare across all documents. TF-IDF = TF × log(N / df).In movie reviews, “terrible” scores higher than “the.”
N-gramsSequences of n consecutive words, capturing some word order.Bigrams of “not very good”: “not very,” “very good.”
Word embeddings (Word2Vec, GloVe, FastText)Dense vectors where similar words are close together.king − man + woman ≈ queen
Contextual embeddings (BERT)Word vectors that change with context.“bank” gets different vectors in “river bank” and “bank loan.”
Hand-crafted featuresFeatures designed by the developer.Text length, number of exclamation marks, sentiment score, POS counts
  • ApplicationThe better the features, the better the model. Good features are often what separates a weak classifier from a strong one.
Part 2 · Step 5 of 8

5. Modeling

  • DefinitionChoosing an algorithm and training it on the features so it learns patterns in the data, then tuning it.
  • Traditional machine learning: Naive Bayes, Logistic Regression, Support Vector Machines, Random Forest. Good for smaller datasets and simple tasks like spam detection.
  • Deep learning: RNN, LSTM, CNN for sequences and text classification.
  • Transformers: BERT, GPT, T5. State-of-the-art; often used by fine-tuning a pretrained model.
  • Good practice: split data into training / validation / test sets (e.g., 70/15/15 or 80/20), tune hyperparameters, and watch for overfitting (memorizes training data) and underfitting (too simple to learn).
  • ExampleTraining a Naive Bayes classifier on TF-IDF features to label customer reviews as positive or negative.
Part 2 · Step 6 of 8

6. Evaluation

Measuring how well the model performs on unseen test data using specific metrics: accuracy, precision, recall, and F1-score.

MetricFormulaMeaning
Accuracy(TP + TN) / (TP + TN + FP + FN)Overall share of correct predictions.
PrecisionTP / (TP + FP)Of everything predicted positive, how many were truly positive.
RecallTP / (TP + FN)Of all truly positive cases, how many the model found.
F1-score2 × (Precision × Recall) / (Precision + Recall)Balance of precision and recall; useful when classes are imbalanced.

TP = true positive, TN = true negative, FP = false positive, FN = false negative.

Worked example (spam detector, 100 emails)

TP = 40, FP = 10, FN = 5, TN = 45.

  • Accuracy = (40 + 45) / 100 = 0.85 (85%)
  • Precision = 40 / (40 + 10) = 0.80
  • Recall = 40 / (40 + 5) ≈ 0.89
  • F1 = 2 × (0.80 × 0.89) / (0.80 + 0.89) ≈ 0.84
Passing rule (from this course reviewer): the model must reach 90% accuracy or higher to move on to the next step. If it falls short, go back and improve the data, preprocessing, features, or model. Remember: accuracy alone can be misleading on imbalanced data (a model that always says “not spam” can score 95% if only 5% of emails are spam), so also check precision, recall, and F1.
Part 2 · Step 7 of 8

7. Deployment

  • DefinitionMaking the trained model available for real use by integrating it into an application or service.
  • ExampleWrapping the model in a REST API (e.g., Flask or FastAPI) and connecting it to a website chatbot, mobile app, or school system, hosted on a server or cloud platform.
  • ConsiderationsResponse time (latency), ability to handle many users (scalability), security, version control, and cost.
Part 2 · Step 8 of 8

8. Monitoring and Model Updating

  • DefinitionContinuously tracking the model’s performance after deployment and updating or retraining it as needed. Language changes over time, so models become outdated (data drift and concept drift).
  • ExampleNew slang, new product names, or trending topics make a sentiment model less accurate. User feedback and error logs reveal failures, and the model is retrained on fresh data.
  • PracticesTrack metrics regularly, collect user feedback, log errors, retrain on new data, test new versions (A/B testing), and keep the ability to roll back to an older version.
Part 2 · Worked Example

End-to-End Example: Sentiment Analysis

StepWhat happens
1. Data GatheringCollect thousands of product reviews from an online store (remove names and contact details).
2. Text Cleaning“OMG!!! Battery sooo good 10/10 http://shop.ph” → remove URL and extra symbols, fix “sooo.”
3. PreprocessingTokenize, lowercase, remove stop words (keep “not”), lemmatize.

8. MonitoringWatch accuracy over time; retrain when new slang or products appear.
